§ i

What is a rotation?

A rotation is a rigid motion that turns every point of a figure around a fixed point called the center of rotation. Distance from the center is preserved — meaning a point that started 5 units from the center is still 5 units from the center after the rotation. The figure itself doesn't change size or shape; it just faces a new direction.

Three things define every rotation
1. Center
The fixed point everything turns around. In this lesson, always the origin (0, 0).
2. Angle
How far to turn. We'll focus on 90° (a quarter turn) and 180° (a half turn).
3. Direction
Counterclockwise (CCW) is positive — the standard mathematical direction. Clockwise (CW) is the reverse.
Heads-up on 180°: a half-turn looks the same whether you go clockwise or counterclockwise — you end up in the exact same place. Direction only matters for 90°.

§ iii

The rules, revealed

Here's what's actually happening, written algebraically. Notice the pattern: 180° flips both signs; 90° rotations swap x and y, and which one becomes negative depends on the direction.

90° CCW
counterclockwise
(x, y) → (−y, x)
(3, 2) → (−2, 3)
180°
half turn (either direction)
(x, y) → (−x, −y)
(3, 2) → (−3, −2)
90° CW
clockwise
(x, y) → (y, −x)
(3, 2) → (2, −3)
How to remember 90°: for both 90° rotations, the x and y swap — that part is the same. The only question is which one gets the negative sign. CCW puts the negative on the new x (originally y); CW puts the negative on the new y (originally x). Or memorize one and remember the other is its reverse.
Why does this work? A rotation about the origin keeps the distance from the origin the same. If you draw the radius from the origin to your point, the radius simply turns by the rotation angle. The coordinate rules above are what that turn looks like written in (x, y) form. You can verify this for yourself: the distance √(x² + y²) is the same before and after.
§ iv

Rotate a whole figure

Now scale up. A figure is just a collection of points connected by edges — so rotating a figure is just rotating each of its vertices and reconnecting them. Drag any vertex to reshape the figure. Choose One vertex mode to isolate a single point's rotation (everything else fades), or Whole figure mode to rotate it all together. Try the same figure at 90° CCW and 180° — see how the shape stays exactly the same, just facing differently.

§ v

How to do this on paper

Three ways to perform a rotation by hand. Pick whichever feels most natural — most students end up using a combination, depending on the problem.

Method 1 — Coordinate Rule (fastest)
1
Write down each vertex's coordinates. If your triangle has vertices A(2, 5), B(4, 1), and C(−1, 3), list them clearly.
2
Apply the rule for the rotation you want:
• 90° CCW: (x, y) → (−y, x)
• 90° CW: (x, y) → (y, −x)
• 180°: (x, y) → (−x, −y)
3
Plot the new points on the same coordinate plane and connect them in the same order as the original. Example: A(2, 5) under 90° CCW becomes A′(−5, 2).
Method 2 — Visual Count from Origin
1
For each vertex, count how many units right/left and up/down it is from the origin. A point at (3, 2) is 3 right and 2 up.
2
Now imagine rotating those directions by your angle. A 90° CCW turn changes "right" into "up", "up" into "left", "left" into "down", "down" into "right". So 3 right + 2 up becomes 3 up + 2 left, landing at (−2, 3).
3
Plot the new vertex. Repeat for every vertex of the figure, then connect them.
Method 3 — Tracing Paper
1
Place tracing paper over your coordinate grid. Trace both the figure and a small "+" mark at the origin.
2
Hold the tracing paper down at the origin with your pencil tip. Rotate the tracing paper itself — 90° (a quarter turn) or 180° (a half turn) — in the correct direction (CCW means the top of the page swings to your left).
3
Press hard at each vertex to mark its new location on the original paper underneath. Lift the tracing paper and connect the new dots.
Don't have tracing paper handy? Use any thin paper (notebook paper held up to a window works), or skip this method — Methods 1 and 2 are enough on their own. Method 3 is most useful when you want to see the rotation happen physically.
§ vi

Practice

Work each problem on your own first. Use scratch paper, the sandboxes above, or just your head. When you're ready to check, click Reveal answer.

Problem 1
Point Q is located at (−3, 7). What are the coordinates of Q′ after a 90° CCW rotation about the origin?
Q′ = (−7, −3)
Apply (x, y) → (−y, x). So (−3, 7) becomes (−(7), −3) = (−7, −3).
Problem 2
Triangle ABC has vertices A(1, 2), B(4, 2), and C(4, 6). It is rotated 180° about the origin. Find the coordinates of A′, B′, and C′.
A′(−1, −2), B′(−4, −2), C′(−4, −6)
Apply (x, y) → (−x, −y) to each vertex — flip both signs.
Problem 3
A point at (6, 0) is rotated about the origin. Its image lands at (0, −6). Which rotation was applied?
90° clockwise
Apply (x, y) → (y, −x): (6, 0) becomes (0, −6). ✓
Sanity check: 90° CCW would have sent (6, 0) to (0, 6), and 180° would have sent it to (−6, 0).
Problem 4
Point R is at (−2, −5). After a 90° CW rotation about the origin, where is R′?
R′ = (−5, 2)
Apply (x, y) → (y, −x): (−2, −5) becomes (−5, −(−2)) = (−5, 2).
Problem 5 — Challenge
A figure is rotated 90° CCW, and then its image is rotated another 90° CCW. What single rotation has the same effect as those two combined?
180° about the origin.
Two consecutive quarter-turns in the same direction = a half-turn. You can verify with coordinates: (x, y) → (−y, x) → (−x, −y), which is exactly the 180° rule.

Summary

Definition
A rotation is a rigid motion that turns every point of a figure around a fixed center by a specified angle and direction. Distance from the center is preserved, so the original figure and its image are always congruent. In this lesson the center is always the origin.
The three rules — at a glance
90° CCW
(x, y) → (−y, x)
180°
(x, y) → (−x, −y)
90° CW
(x, y) → (y, −x)
Big takeaways
  • Every rotation is defined by three things: a center, an angle, and a direction.
  • 180° looks the same in either direction. 90° doesn't.
  • A rotation preserves distance, angles, and shape — only orientation changes.
  • The figure and its image are always congruent.
  • To rotate by hand: apply the coordinate rule to every vertex, then plot.
